Where This Comes From

Introduction

In 1985, former Vassar and Bucknell admissions dean Richard Moll coined a term that would reshape how prospective students and their families thought about public higher education: the "Public Ivies." In his book of the same name, Moll identified a set of state universities that, in his estimation, delivered an academic experience rivaling the private Ivy League institutions of the American Northeast — but at a fraction of the cost, and open to a far broader swath of the public.

Moll's central argument was simple but, at the time, somewhat provocative: prestige was not the exclusive property of small, private, tuition-driven colleges. A handful of large state flagship universities had, through decades of sustained public investment, assembled faculties, research infrastructure, and student bodies that could compete with — and in some disciplines exceed — their private peers. These institutions accomplished this while still fulfilling a fundamentally different mission than the Ivies: educating tens of thousands of students annually, many of them first-generation college attendees, at a public price point.

Four decades later, the landscape of American higher education has shifted considerably. Endowments have grown, research budgets have ballooned into the billions, admissions have become dramatically more selective, and the competition for top faculty and graduate talent has intensified. Yet the core tension Moll identified — the balancing act between elite academic output and public accessibility — remains as relevant as ever.

But the "Public Ivy" label itself has drifted a long way from its original meaning. Moll's list was tight and deliberately selective. In the decades since, the term has been recycled, expanded, and diluted by successive guidebooks and rankings sites, to the point that the most commonly cited modern version of the list now includes somewhere in the neighborhood of thirty schools. That is not a badge of exclusivity — it is closer to a description of "most flagship state universities," which defeats the purpose of the comparison in the first place.

The actual Ivy League, by contrast, has exactly eight members. Its prestige is inseparable from its exclusivity: the label means something precisely because so few schools carry it. If the "Public Ivy" comparison is going to mean anything similar, it needs to observe the same discipline. Rather than trimming down the existing, thirty-school version of the list, this piece takes a different approach: building an entirely new, independent list from scratch, selecting only the state flagships whose combination of research output, faculty caliber, selectivity, and alumni influence puts them in a class by themselves. What follows is a "Modern Public Ivies" in the strictest sense: not a pared-down version of someone else's list, but a short list of the best of the best, assembled fresh.

How We Built A List Of 8

Our Methodology

Building a list of eight from scratch requires actual criteria, not vibes. We evaluated candidate state flagships against four measurable pillars, then kept only the schools that cleared the bar on all four at once. A school could be a research powerhouse but lack elite selectivity, or have great name recognition but a thinner research budget — those schools didn't make the cut. Only the ones that stack up across the board made the final eight.

1

Research Scale

Annual research expenditure in the billion-dollar range, reflecting real federal, state, and private research infrastructure — not just prestige by association.

2

Faculty Caliber

Presence of Nobel laureates, National Academy members, and other top-tier faculty honors, concentrated enough to shape a department's global reputation.

3

Selectivity & Outcomes

Admissions selectivity and graduate outcomes — employment, graduate school placement, and earnings — that hold up against elite private institutions.

4

Alumni Influence

A large, engaged alumni network with measurable reach across industries, government, and philanthropy — scale that most private schools can't match.

⚠️ A note on rigor: this is a qualitative, editorially-driven exercise, not a statistical ranking model. We're not claiming these four criteria produce a single "correct" list — we're using them as a discipline to force genuine exclusivity, the same way the real Ivy League's membership is fixed rather than fluid.

The List

School Profiles

University of California, Berkeley

Founded 1868
📍 Berkeley, California Berkeley Blue & California Gold

UC Berkeley is routinely ranked as the top public research university in the United States. Its academic reputation spans an unusually wide range of fields, from a top-ranked College of Engineering and Haas School of Business to a physics department with deep roots in the history of nuclear science and a rich tradition in the humanities and social sciences. Berkeley's faculty roster includes numerous Nobel laureates, MacArthur "genius grant" recipients, and members of the National Academy of Sciences. Its role at the center of the University of California system, combined with proximity to Silicon Valley, has made it a critical pipeline for technology, biotechnology, and public policy talent nationally and globally.

University of California, Los Angeles

Founded 1919
📍 Los Angeles, California UCLA Blue & Gold

UCLA has grown from a regional teachers' college into one of the most applied-to universities in the country, drawing more freshman applications annually than nearly any other institution in the nation. Its academic strengths are broad and deep: a top film school in Theater, Film and Television; a highly ranked David Geffen School of Medicine; strong programs in engineering, psychology, and political science; and an athletics program with one of the most decorated histories in collegiate sports. UCLA's location within Los Angeles gives it distinctive strength in entertainment, media, and entertainment law, alongside a substantial biomedical research enterprise anchored by its medical center.

University of Michigan

Founded 1817
📍 Ann Arbor, Michigan Maize & Blue

The University of Michigan is one of the oldest and most comprehensive public research universities in the country, with top-ranked programs spanning its Ross School of Business, College of Engineering, Law School, and School of Public Health. Michigan consistently ranks among the leading recipients of federal research funding, with particular strength in engineering, information science, and social science research through institutions like the Institute for Social Research. Its enormous and famously loyal alumni network, paired with a storied athletics tradition, gives graduates an unusually dense set of professional connections across nearly every industry and region of the country.

University of Virginia

Founded 1819
📍 Charlottesville, Virginia Orange & Blue

Founded by Thomas Jefferson and built around his architectural vision of an "Academical Village," UVA blends a distinctive undergraduate liberal arts culture with strong professional programs, including a highly regarded McIntire School of Commerce, Darden School of Business, and School of Law. UVA has long been noted for combining high selectivity and strong graduation outcomes with a public price tag, and it consistently posts among the best return-on-investment figures of any public university. Its honor code and student self-governance traditions remain distinctive parts of its academic culture.

University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill

Founded 1789
📍 Chapel Hill, North Carolina Carolina Blue & Navy

UNC Chapel Hill holds the distinction of being the first public university in the United States to enroll students and confer degrees. Today it anchors the Research Triangle alongside Duke and NC State, giving it substantial strength in biomedical and public health research through its Gillings School of Global Public Health and School of Medicine. UNC also maintains a highly regarded Kenan-Flagler Business School and a journalism school with a long tradition of producing working professionals. Its combination of research output, accessibility to in-state students, and athletic tradition has made it a perennial fixture near the top of public university rankings.

University of Texas at Austin

Founded 1883
📍 Austin, Texas Burnt Orange & Charcoal

UT Austin combines an enormous endowment — bolstered by the Permanent University Fund's oil and gas revenues — with a rapidly growing reputation in engineering, computer science, and business, aided considerably by its location in one of the country's fastest-growing technology hubs. Its Cockrell School of Engineering and McCombs School of Business are both nationally ranked, and the university has become an increasingly important feeder for Texas's booming semiconductor and technology sectors. UT Austin's scale — one of the largest single-campus enrollments in the country — allows it to sustain an unusually wide breadth of top-tier academic departments simultaneously.

University of Washington

Founded 1861
📍 Seattle, Washington Purple & Gold

The University of Washington is a dominant regional research power with a global footprint, consistently ranking among the top recipients of federal research funding in the country, particularly in medicine, public health, and computer science. Its School of Medicine and Foster School of Business are nationally regarded, and its Paul G. Allen School of Computer Science & Engineering has become one of the most influential programs in the country, feeding directly into the Seattle-area tech ecosystem anchored by Amazon and Microsoft. UW's location has made it an increasingly essential pipeline for technology and biomedical talent on the West Coast, giving it an outsized institutional influence relative to its size.

College of William & Mary

Founded 1693
📍 Williamsburg, Virginia Green & Gold

The second-oldest institution of higher education in the United States, William & Mary occupies a unique niche among the Public Ivies: a small, highly selective public liberal arts university rather than a sprawling research flagship. Its history is deeply intertwined with the American founding — alumni include Thomas Jefferson and James Monroe — and it maintains a strong tradition in government, law (home to the nation's oldest law school), and the humanities. William & Mary demonstrates that the "public ivy" model need not require enormous scale, offering an intimate undergraduate experience alongside meaningful research output.

Beyond The Rankings

How Each One Put Itself On The Map

Rankings and research dollars make the statistical case, but every school on this list also has its own singular claim to fame — a specific moment or achievement that defines it in the public imagination, independent of any ranking system.

⚛️

UC Berkeley

Physicists at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory have discovered more chemical elements than any other institution in the world, including berkelium and californium — both literally named after the school and its home state.

💻

UCLA

The first message ever sent over ARPANET — the direct ancestor of the modern internet — was transmitted from a UCLA computer lab in October 1969, making the campus the literal birthplace of networked computing.

🕊️

Michigan

In a 2 a.m. speech on the steps of the Michigan Union in October 1960, presidential candidate John F. Kennedy first floated the idea that would become the Peace Corps.

🏛️

UVA

Thomas Jefferson's "Academical Village" design for the Lawn and Rotunda is one of the only university campuses in the world to be designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site, alongside sites like the Taj Mahal and the Great Wall.

🚀

UNC Chapel Hill

UNC's Morehead Planetarium secretly trained every Apollo astronaut, including Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin, in celestial navigation before they ever set foot on the Moon.

🖥️

UT Austin

The Texas Advanced Computing Center has operated some of the most powerful supercomputers ever run by a university, including Frontera and Vista, putting UT Austin at the center of the nation's high-performance computing infrastructure.

🚣

University of Washington

The 1936 UW rowing team's gold medal at the Berlin Olympics — the true story behind The Boys in the Boat — put the university on the national map decades before its research reputation caught up.

🔑

William & Mary

Phi Beta Kappa, the nation's oldest and most prestigious academic honor society, was founded at William & Mary in 1776, the same year the country itself was founded.

The Hard Part

Enforcing a strict cap of eight required difficult omissions. The following world-class institutions sit directly on the bubble and represent our official honorable mentions — schools that clear several of our criteria convincingly, but not all four at once.

University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (UIUC)

The Case

Grainger College of Engineering and UIUC's CS program are elite global titans, ranked Top 5 alongside MIT and Stanford.

Why It Missed

Uneven general undergraduate selectivity across humanities and liberal arts compared to Michigan or UVA.

The Case

Elite admissions selectivity (roughly 13%) and engineering prowess that competes directly with MIT and Caltech.

Why It Missed

Operates primarily as a polytechnic specialist rather than a comprehensive university with medical and liberal arts depth.

3

University of Florida

The Case

A surging flagship that has leaped into the Top 6 US News public rankings, backed by high selectivity (roughly 23%) and huge state funding.

Why It Missed

Still building the century-old national brand clout enjoyed by regional peers UVA and UNC Chapel Hill.

4

University of Wisconsin–Madison

The Case

A massive research juggernaut with a multi-billion-dollar budget and world-class faculty.

Why It Missed

Edged out by direct regional counterpart Michigan in the Big Ten.

Common Threads

Key Pillars

What Sustains the Modern Public Ivy

  • Billion-Dollar Research Budgets Each of these institutions now operates research enterprises exceeding a billion dollars annually, funded through a mix of federal grants, state appropriations, and private partnerships, enabling large-scale work in medicine, engineering, and the sciences that smaller private institutions simply cannot match in scale.
  • World-Class Faculty Decades of sustained investment have allowed these universities to recruit and retain faculty at the very top of their fields, including Nobel laureates, National Academy members, and leading voices across the humanities and professional schools.
  • Expansive Alumni Networks Because these universities graduate far larger classes than their private peers, their alumni networks are correspondingly vast, creating dense professional and philanthropic connections that span virtually every industry, region, and level of seniority.
  • Strong Graduate Outcomes Across employment placement, graduate and professional school admission, and long-term earnings, these universities post outcomes that rival elite private institutions, while serving a student body many times larger and considerably more socioeconomically diverse.

Taken together, the pattern holds across every criterion we set out to test. Three of these eight — Berkeley, UCLA, and Michigan — are ranked by US News as the top three public universities in the country, full stop. Michigan and UCLA post national research expenditure ranks (#2 and #4 respectively) ahead of several individual Ivy League schools. UVA's out-of-state admission rate of roughly 10% is tighter than Cornell's overall acceptance rate. And UNC's out-of-state rate of roughly 8%, driven by a state-mandated enrollment cap, is competitive with Brown's. This is not a list of "very good state schools" — it is a list of institutions that are, by the numbers, operating in the same tier as the private Ivy League, not merely adjacent to it.

📊 On precision: figures above are rounded and change year to year — acceptance rates shift with each admissions cycle, rankings are refreshed annually by US News and Niche, and research expenditures are reported annually via the NSF's HERD survey. Treat these as directionally accurate snapshots that support the argument, not a live scoreboard.
Wrapping Up

Conclusion

Richard Moll's original "Public Ivies" concept was, at its core, an argument about access: that academic excellence did not need to be rationed exclusively through small, expensive, private institutions. Four decades later, the Modern Public Ivies demonstrate that this argument has only strengthened with time. As the cost of private higher education continues to climb and public skepticism about the value of a college degree grows, these flagship universities are increasingly positioned as the standard-bearers of a higher education model that is simultaneously excellent and accessible. The map makes the point visually: the real Ivy League is a tight cluster of eight campuses in a single corner of the country, while the Modern Public Ivies stretch from the Pacific Northwest to the Texas hill country to the Virginia tidewater. That reach is no accident. These universities exist to serve the people of their states, so their excellence is spread across the country instead of concentrated in one region.

Looking forward, the role of these institutions is likely to become even more consequential. As state governments and the federal government grapple with questions of research funding, affordability, and workforce development, public flagships sit at the intersection of all three. Their continued ability to attract world-class faculty, produce cutting-edge research, and graduate large, capable cohorts of students at a fraction of private-school cost suggests that the "Public Ivy" model — far from a relic of 1985 — may be one of the more durable and important institutional structures in American higher education's future.
